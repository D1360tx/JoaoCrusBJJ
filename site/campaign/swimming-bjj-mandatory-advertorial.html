Why Swimming and Jiu-Jitsu Are Mandatory in Our Family

They look like two different activities. Under pressure, both teach a child the same deeper lesson: stay calm, understand what is happening, and find the next move.

Campaign editorial draft 8 minute read

There is a point in parenting when you realize protection cannot only mean being present.

When children are small, we do almost everything for them. We hold the hand. Check the water. Scan the playground. Step between them and the thing that feels too big.

But childhood keeps widening. There are pool parties, school hallways, sports practices, unfamiliar rooms, and moments when a child feels physically or emotionally overwhelmed. We will not be standing beside them every time.

That is why our family separates activities into two categories. There are activities that are fun, healthy, or interesting. Then there are skills we believe a child should have before they truly need them.

Swimming and jiu-jitsu belong in the second category.

Calling them mandatory is not a claim that every family must make the same decision. It is the standard we chose for ours.

Swimming gives a child practice becoming capable in water. Jiu-jitsu gives a child practice staying present when another body, a difficult position, or a surge of emotion creates pressure.

The hidden problem is not pressure. It is having no practiced response.

Parents naturally want to remove danger, fear, conflict, and frustration. We should remove what is unnecessary. But a childhood with no pressure is not possible, and it would not prepare a child for much.

The better question is: when pressure arrives, has my child felt a safe version of it before?

Have they practiced stopping? Breathing? Communicating a boundary? Listening to a clear instruction? Protecting their body? Solving one problem at a time? Resetting after the first attempt fails?

These are not lessons children absorb because an adult explains them once. They become available through repetition.

What “mandatory” means here

This is an editorial family belief, not a promise that either activity prevents every accident, conflict, or injury. The value is in age-appropriate instruction and repeated practice.

Why these two activities belong together.

Swimming and jiu-jitsu do not use the same movements. They do not solve the same external problem. But they both ask a child to build an internal sequence under pressure.

First, notice what is happening. Then control the immediate reaction. Use the body with purpose. Follow a practiced response. If it does not work, adjust.

That is the part many parents are actually looking for when they say they want their child to be confident.

Confidence is not the promise that nothing difficult will happen. It is the memory of meeting difficulty before and discovering, “I have something I can do next.”

The shared mechanism

Different environments. A similar internal sequence.

Neither skill should be reduced to fear. Both can be taught as competence: calm first, then a practiced response.

01

In the water

  • Orient to the environment
  • Manage breath and immediate panic
  • Use practiced body position and movement
  • Move toward the next safe action
02

On the mat

  • Recognize pressure and position
  • Use a shared stop signal when needed
  • Protect the body and create space
  • Listen, solve, reset, and try again
Composure becomes easier to access when it has been practiced.

The goal is not a fearless child. The goal is a child who can feel discomfort without losing every available option.
