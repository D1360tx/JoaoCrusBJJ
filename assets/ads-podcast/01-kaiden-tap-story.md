# Concept 01: Kaiden, the Tap, and Earned Confidence

**Status:** Working  
**Created:** 2026-07-24  
**Source:** Diego's firsthand parent story  
**Primary formats:** Podcast episode, Meta video ad, short-form clips, parent email  
**Primary destination:** Toddler / Little Champions campaign page

## 1. Why this story matters

The visible result is confidence, but the mechanism is more specific:

> Kaiden learned that he could tap. Tapping gave him control. Control helped him feel safe. Feeling safe gave him the confidence to keep learning.

This supports the campaign themes of safe boundaries, body autonomy, emotional regulation, and confidence earned through real experiences.

## 2. Source story

When Kaiden first started Jiu-Jitsu, he was scared. He would often cry during practice with the other kids. He eventually told Diego that he did not want to go anymore.

Diego asked why. Kaiden answered, "My friends hurt me."

That answer helped Diego understand that Kaiden was not rejecting Jiu-Jitsu itself. He felt uncomfortable during practice and did not yet understand that he had a safe way to stop.

Diego explained that whenever Kaiden felt uncomfortable, frustrated, or stuck, he could tap. Tapping tells a training partner to stop and allows the child to safely leave the position.

After that conversation, Kaiden's experience changed. During the next few classes, Diego saw him tap when he felt stuck instead of becoming overwhelmed. Kaiden began to feel safe, confident, and excited to continue learning.

Kaiden started swimming at 6 months old, soccer at age 2, and Jiu-Jitsu at age 3. Diego and his wife believe important life skills should begin early, while children are still becoming comfortable with new experiences. For their family, swimming and Jiu-Jitsu are non-negotiable life skills.

## 4. Podcast episode structure

### Working title

**Why We Teach Three-Year-Olds to Tap Before We Teach Them to Win**

### Opening

Joao explains why most schools start at age 4 and why his Little Champions program starts at age 3 on purpose.

### Diego's story

1. Kaiden cried during practice rounds.
2. He said, "My friends hurt me."
3. Diego realized Kaiden did not understand his own ability to stop.
4. Diego explained the tap.
5. Kaiden began using it when he felt uncomfortable.
6. His experience changed from fear and frustration to confidence.

### Joao's teaching perspective

Suggested prompts:

- What does a three-year-old understand about physical discomfort?
- How do you teach tapping without making a child more fearful?
- How does tapping teach both sides of a boundary?
- What should a coach do when a child cries or freezes?
- How do short games and simple instructions help young children regulate?
- What should parents expect during the first few classes?
- Why is a child who watches before joining not failing?

### Parent philosophy

Diego explains why his family starts swimming, sports, and Jiu-Jitsu early. Frame swimming and Jiu-Jitsu as the family's two non-negotiable life skills.

### Closing

Joao connects the story to the program:

> We do not expect a three-year-old to arrive fearless. We teach them how to feel safe, communicate a boundary, and try again.

## 6. Draft short-form scripts

### 60-second podcast cut

**Diego:** When Kaiden first started Jiu-Jitsu at age 3, he would sometimes cry during practice with the other kids. Then he told me he did not want to go anymore.

I asked him why, and he said, "My friends hurt me."

That was when I realized he did not understand that he had control. I explained that whenever he felt uncomfortable or stuck, he could tap. Tap means stop.

Over the next few classes, I watched him use it. Instead of getting overwhelmed, he tapped, reset, and tried again.

Giving him permission to tap made him feel safe. Feeling safe gave him the confidence to keep learning. Now he loves Jiu-Jitsu.

**Joao:** That is exactly why teaching a three-year-old requires a different approach. We teach them how to communicate, how to respect a boundary, and how to try again safely.

### 30-second parent-story ad

When my son started Jiu-Jitsu at age 3, he would cry during practice. He told me, "My friends hurt me."

Then I realized he did not understand that he could tap. I explained that tap means stop.

Once he knew he had control, everything changed. He felt safe enough to keep trying, and that is where his confidence began.

My three-year-old wanted to quit Jiu-Jitsu. He did not need a speech about being tough. He needed to understand one thing: tap means stop. Control made him feel safe, and safety gave him confidence.

## 10. Claim and safety guardrails

- Present this as Diego and Kaiden's experience, not a guaranteed result.
- Explain that "My friends hurt me" was Kaiden's description before he understood tapping. Do not imply that the academy ignored unsafe behavior.
- Say that tapping communicates stop. Do not claim it eliminates all injury risk.
- Use "practice with other kids" before introducing the BJJ term "rolling."
- Frame swimming and Jiu-Jitsu as non-negotiable for Diego's family, not for every family.
- Confirm permission before using Kaiden's name, image, voice, or identifiable footage in paid advertising.
- Do not promise a specific trial structure, price, guarantee, or uniform.
- Do not use em dashes.
